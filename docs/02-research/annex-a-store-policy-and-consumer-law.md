## 1. Auto-renewing subscription / paywall disclosure requirements

**FACTS — Apple**
- Guideline 3.1.2(c): "Before asking a customer to subscribe, you should clearly describe what the user will get for the price," and apps must communicate the Schedule 2 (Developer Program License Agreement) requirements: length of renewal term and that it continues until canceled, what is provided each period, the actual charges billed, and how to cancel. Source: App Store Review Guidelines, https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ — verified from primary source.
- Guideline 3.1.2(a): subscriptions must provide ongoing value, last at least 7 days, work across the user's devices; "Apps that attempt to scam users will be removed… includes apps that attempt to trick users into purchasing a subscription under false pretenses or engage in bait-and-switch and scam practices." Same URL — verified from primary source.
- The guidelines require a restore mechanism for restorable in-app purchases (auto-renewable subscriptions are restorable). Same URL (section 3.1 IAP rules) — verified from primary source (exact subsection as summarized from the page: 3.1.1/3.1.2(a)).
- Apple's subscription sign-up screen requirements: must include subscription name and duration, content/services provided, "Full renewal price, shown clearly and prominently," and "A way for current subscribers to sign in or restore purchases." Also: "In the purchase flow, the amount that will be billed must be the most prominent pricing element in the layout… This ensures that people are not misled" — equivalent per-week/month breakdown prices must be "in a subordinate position and size." Free trials must state trial length and the price billed after. Source: Apple, Auto-renewable subscriptions, https://developer.apple.com/app-store/subscriptions/ — verified from primary source.

**FACTS — Google Play**
- Subscriptions policy: "You must be transparent about your offer," including "clearly and explicitly disclosing your offer terms, the cost of your subscription, the frequency of your billing cycle" and whether a subscription is required to use the app; users must not have to perform additional action to see this; apps must clearly disclose how a subscriber can cancel and/or manage a subscription; free trials/intro offers must clearly say how to cancel before conversion. Source: Google Play Console Help, "Subscriptions" policy, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9900533 — primary policy text obtained via search snippet; direct fetch blocked (403).
- Google Play has no separate "restore purchases" rule; Play Billing associates subscriptions with the Google account across devices. Source: Android Developers, About subscriptions, https://developer.android.com/google/play/billing/subscriptions — secondary confirmation via search results; not deeply verified.

## 2. Deceptive design / dark patterns / countdown pricing

**FACTS — Apple**
- Guideline 2.3.1(a): marketing an app in a misleading way, "such as by promoting content or services that it does not actually offer… or promoting a false price, whether within or outside of the App Store, is grounds for removal of your app from the App Store… and termination of your developer account." Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ — verified from primary source.
- Guideline 3.1.2(a) (quoted above) bans subscription tricks/bait-and-switch. Verified from primary source.
- No Apple guideline explicitly names countdown timers; the applicable hooks are "false price" (2.3.1) and "false pretenses" (3.1.2(a)). Verified from primary source (absence checked in fetched guideline text).

**FACTS — Google Play**
- Deceptive Behavior / Misleading Claims policy: Google Play "doesn't allow apps that contain false or misleading information or claims"; apps "must provide accurate disclosures… and must perform as reasonably and accurately expected by the user." Sources: Google Play Deceptive Behavior policy, https://play.google.com/about/privacy-security-deception/deceptive-behavior/ and consolidated Developer Program Policy, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16810878 — primary content via search snippets; direct fetch blocked.
- The Subscriptions policy transparency wording ("clearly and accurately describe" offer terms) is the store's main lever against misleading paywall offers; no Google Play policy text specifically naming countdown timers was found. Confidence: could not verify a countdown-specific rule (fetch blocked); treated as absent.

**FACTS — UK (DMCC Act 2024)**
- The DMCC Act's unfair commercial practices regime came into force 6 April 2025, giving the CMA direct enforcement powers (fines reported up to 10% of global turnover). Sources: Browne Jacobson, https://www.brownejacobson.com/insights/retail-law-roundup-may-2025/unfair-commercial-practices ; CMS, https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/2025-uk-consumer-law-round-up — secondary sources (consistent across multiple law firms).
- DMCC Schedule 20 banned practice: "Falsely stating that a product will only be available for a limited time, or that it will only be available on particular terms for a limited time, in order to elicit an immediate decision and deprive consumers of sufficient opportunity or time to make an informed choice" — the word "very" (from the old 2008 CPRs) was removed, widening the ban. Sources: legislation.gov.uk Schedule 20, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2024/13/schedule/20 (fetch blocked) ; Ashurst, https://www.ashurst.com/en/insights/new-era-for-consumer-enforcement-in-the-uk/ — statutory text via search snippet + secondary corroboration.
- CMA guidance CMA207 "Unfair commercial practices" covers misleading urgency/countdown clocks and bans drip pricing (invitation to purchase must include the total price incl. mandatory fees). Source: gov.uk, https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices (fetch blocked); corroborated by Reed Smith, https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/four-months-into-the-dmcc-navigating-cma-guidance-and-what-to-expect/ — secondary.
- Enforcement is real: on 18 November 2025 the CMA opened its first eight consumer-law investigations under DMCC powers, targeting drip pricing, "misleading time-limited offers" and pressure selling. Sources: Taylor Wessing, https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/12/aq-cma-announces-first-eight-investigations-into-consumer-law-breaches-using-new-enforcement-powers ; Baker Botts, https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2025/november/cma-launches-consumer-protection-drive — secondary sources.
- Precedent: CMA secured a High Court order against Emma Sleep over misleading sales promotions (incl. countdown-timer/discount tactics). Source: TLT LLP, https://www.tlt.com/insights-and-events/insight/cma-secures-high-court-order-against-emma-sleep-over-misleading-sales-promotions — secondary source.

**FACTS — EU**
- UCPD (2005/29/EC) Annex I item 7 bans falsely stating a product is available only for a limited time to deprive consumers of an informed choice; the Commission's 2021 UCPD Guidance §4.2.7 confirms dark patterns (incl. fake urgency/countdown timers) fall under UCPD Articles 5–9. Source: Commission Guidance (2021/C 526/01), https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52021XC1229(06) — primary reference located; content confirmed via secondary summaries (Freshfields, https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/technology-quotient/digital-fairness-fitness-checkpart-1-dark-patterns-102jpiy).
- Price Indication Directive Art. 6a (Omnibus): any price-reduction announcement must state the "prior price" = lowest price in the previous 30 days, to prevent fake discounts; CJEU confirmed strict reading in 2024 (Aldi Süd). Sources: EUR-Lex Art. 6a guidance, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX:52021XC1229(06)&from=EN ; Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, https://www.hsfkramer.com/notes/tmt/2024-posts/consumer-update-cjeu-confirms-that-price-reduction-claims-must-be-based-on-lowest-price-in-last-30-days — primary reference + secondary.
- DSA Article 25 bans dark patterns ("false urgency messages or countdown timers" cited as examples) but applies to **online platforms** and expressly not to practices already covered by UCPD/GDPR. Sources: DSA Library Art. 25, https://dsa-library.com/article/25/ ; Mason Hayes & Curran, https://www.mhc.ie/latest/insights/transparency-under-the-eu-digital-services-act — secondary sources.
- An EU "Digital Fairness Act" targeting dark patterns (fake countdowns named in EP material) is in preparation/proposal stage in 2025–2026, not yet law. Sources: European Parliament briefing, https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/ATAG/2025/767191/EPRS_ATA(2025)767191_EN.pdf ; Osborne Clarke, https://www.osborneclarke.com/insights/digital-fairness-act-unpacked-dark-patterns — secondary; exact legislative status as of July 2026 could not be verified.

## 3. Health & fitness content rules

**FACTS — Apple**
- Guideline 1.4.1: medical apps that could provide inaccurate data "may be reviewed with greater scrutiny"; "Apps must clearly disclose data and methodology to support accuracy claims relating to health measurements, and if the level of accuracy or methodology cannot be validated, we will reject your app" (e.g., claiming to measure blood pressure/glucose with device sensors is not permitted); "Apps should remind users to check with a doctor in addition to using the app and before making medical decisions." Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ — verified from primary source.

**FACTS — Google Play**
- Health Content and Services policy bans misleading health claims that contradict medical consensus or can cause harm, and requires regulatory proof or disclaimers for medical functionality. Current policy page: https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511 — primary content via search snippets; direct fetch blocked.
- Since 31 August 2024 all health-related apps must complete an accurate **Health apps declaration** in Play Console disclosing supported health features (categories include fitness, nutrition, sleep, mental health, medical, etc.). Sources: Health apps declaration form, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14738291 ; Health app categories, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13996367 — primary URLs located, content via snippets/secondary.
- A major Health Content and Services update took effect **28 August 2025** (broader scope covering wellness/fitness apps, medical transparency, health-data handling), with further requirements from **January 2026** (medical-device labeling, stricter Health Connect justifications, ban on using age-restricted signals for health profiling). Sources: My App Monitor, https://myappmonitor.com/blog/google-play-policy-update-for-health-apps and https://myappmonitor.com/blog/google-play-health-apps-update-2026-requirements ; Play policy announcement Jul 10 2025, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16296680 — secondary sources (Google pages blocked); dates consistent across sources.

## 4. User photos of body/skin as sensitive data

**FACTS — Apple**
- Privacy nutrition labels (App Privacy Details) require declaring all collected data; categories include "Health & Fitness" ("any other user provided health or medical data") and "Photos or Videos" under User Content; "tracking" = linking app data with third-party data for targeted advertising or sharing with data brokers. Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/app-privacy-details/ — verified from primary source.
- Guideline 5.1.2(vi): data from HealthKit, Clinical Health Records, "depth and/or facial mapping tools (e.g. ARKit, Camera APIs, or Photo APIs) may not be used for marketing, advertising or use-based data mining, including by third parties." Guideline 5.1.3: health/fitness data may not be used for advertising/marketing; apps "may not store personal health information in iCloud." Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ — verified from primary source.

**FACTS — Google Play**
- Data safety form (required since 20 July 2022) has explicit data types "Health and fitness" and "Photos or videos"; every collection/sharing purpose (incl. advertising) must be declared. Source: https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10787469 — primary URL, content via snippets/secondary (TermsFeed, https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/google-data-safety-form/).
- User Data policy: apps collecting "personal and sensitive user data" (health data is listed) must give prominent in-app disclosure and obtain consent, and limit use to declared purposes. Source: consolidated Developer Program Policy, https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16810878 — primary content via snippets; direct fetch blocked.

## 5. Account deletion

**FACTS — Apple**
- Guideline 5.1.1(v): "If your app supports account creation, you must also offer account deletion within the app." Source: guidelines URL above — verified from primary source.
- Apple support detail (in force since 30 June 2022): deletion option must be easy to find; must "delete the entire account record, along with associated personal data" — deactivation alone is insufficient; non-regulated apps "should not require people to make a phone call, send an email, or go through other support flows." Source: https://developer.apple.com/support/offering-account-deletion-in-your-app/ — verified from primary source.

**FACTS — Google Play**
- Account deletion policy: apps allowing account creation must offer a "readily discoverable" in-app deletion option AND a web link (declared in the Data safety form) enabling account and data deletion without reinstalling the app; data-deletion answers were required in the Data safety form from 7 Dec 2023, enforcement (incl. removal) from 31 May 2024. Source: https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13327111 — primary URL, content via snippets + secondary (TermsFeed, https://www.termsfeed.com/blog/google-data-safety-form-delete-account-url/).

## INTERPRETATION (clearly separated from facts above)

**The Page-4 pattern (countdown discount at first launch, "offer only lasts n hours, then back to normal price") — risk assessment:**

1. **Apple:** Countdown paywalls per se are common on iOS and not explicitly banned; rejection risk comes from the offer being *false*. If the "normal price" is rarely or never charged, or the countdown resets/re-appears, this is squarely "promoting a false price" (2.3.1 — grounds for removal and account termination) and "purchasing a subscription under false pretenses" (3.1.2(a)). Also, App Review requires the billed renewal amount to be the most prominent price element — a design where the discount framing dominates and the renewal price is small print is a documented rejection pattern. Moderate rejection risk at review; high enforcement risk if discovered fake post-launch.
2. **Google Play:** same logic under Subscriptions transparency ("clearly and accurately describe" the offer) and Deceptive Behavior/Misleading Claims. Google's review is more automated, so launch rejection is less likely, but user complaints can trigger policy strikes up to removal.
3. **UK — highest legal risk:** if the discount is not genuinely time-limited (timer resets, offer perpetually available, or the "normal price" is artificial), it is a *per se banned practice* under DMCC Schedule 20 (false limited-time availability), enforceable directly by the CMA since 6 April 2025 with fines up to ~10% of global turnover — and the CMA's very first DMCC investigations (Nov 2025) target exactly "misleading time-limited offers." A GBP-pricing, UK-founded app is a natural CMA target class. A *genuine* one-time intro offer honestly framed (real deadline, real standard price actually charged afterwards, honoured once) is lawful.
4. **EU:** fake urgency breaches UCPD (Annex I item 7 / Arts 6–8); a "reduced price" claim invites Art. 6a PID-style scrutiny of the reference price (note: Art. 6a formally covers goods, but misleading reference pricing for services is still caught by UCPD). DSA Art. 25 likely does not apply to a single-vendor app (it targets platforms), so UCPD is the operative law. The upcoming Digital Fairness Act is expected to name fake countdown timers explicitly.
5. **Practical read:** the compliant version of Page 4 is: a real introductory offer (both stores support intro/promotional pricing natively), shown with full renewal price most prominent, real expiry, no timer reset, and the standard price genuinely charged otherwise. The non-compliant version — evergreen "n hours left" timer at every first launch with a fabricated anchor price — risks App Store removal (2.3.1), Play policy strikes, and direct CMA enforcement.

**Health/fitness angles for this app:** weight loss, skincare and teeth-whitening plans make it a "health" app for Google's Health apps declaration (fitness/nutrition/wellness categories) — expect the declaration to be mandatory and marketing claims to be held to the "no claims contradicting medical consensus" bar. On Apple, tracking-only functionality is low risk under 1.4.1, but outcome claims ("whiter teeth in 12 weeks", "proven ways") need supporting methodology or softer wording, plus a see-a-doctor disclaimer for weight-loss content. Page 6's "based on books and researches" intent helps only if claims are actually cited in-app.

**Body/skin progress photos:** if used to track weight loss/skin/teeth, treat them as health-related user content: declare "Photos or Videos" (+ "Health & Fitness" if any health data) in Apple's privacy label and Google's Data safety form; never feed them to advertising (hard ban under Apple 5.1.2(vi)/5.1.3, and a declared-purpose/ prominent-disclosure problem on Google); both stores require full in-app account deletion plus (Google) a web deletion link — plan the backend for true data erasure from day one.
