# Payment and Trust Evidence (Public User-Voice)

**Status:** Stage 1A synthesis — Q8 (paywall/subscription distrust), Q9 (why people pay for guidance), Q14 (lengths and payment periods users understand). Rows: [corpus](public-evidence-corpus.md) §E3 (55 rows). Interpretation labelled; limitations per [research-limitations.md](research-limitations.md).

## Q8 — What breeds paywall and subscription distrust

1. **The trial that isn't free is the most trust-destroying mechanism captured:** charged immediately, before trial end, or into multi-month bundles never understood (E3-01…03, 09…11 — recurring review theme). The anger is about **consent, not price**.
2. **"Charged after I cancelled" is the severity ceiling** (E3-04…08, 12, 13): billing surviving an explicit cancel converts annoyance into fraud accusations and regulator complaints.
3. **Paywall-after-quiz rage is a distinct recurring theme** (E3-07, 08, 22…24): users prepay effort and personal disclosure, hit a paywall, and — when they do pay — find the "personalised" plan generic. Effort-then-paywall magnifies betrayal. *(Direct confirmation of our suitability-screen honesty rules and of keeping screening purposeful — and a caution for the post-signup survey: everything optional, nothing resembling a quiz-gate.)*
4. **Cancellation asymmetry is read as intent, not incompetence** (certified-mail gyms, email-only cancellation, retention gauntlets; the Amazon "Iliad Flow" settlement gives users the vocabulary — E3-05, 15, 16, 18). Advance-notice renewal reminders are the cheap trust asset (the one praised counter-example, E3-26) — our E9 commitment, externally validated.
5. **Pricing games teach users the sticker price is fake** (dynamic quiz pricing, cancel-to-get-a-discount, $1-entry bait — E3-08, 21, 17): distrust spills onto the whole category — the contamination risk (M6) our honest-pricing rules answer.
6. **Subscription fatigue is background radiation** (E3-18, 19): a real constituency pre-rejects any recurring bill and wants ownership. *(Supports keeping M1 one-off purchase live in the option set.)*
7. **Hidden add-ons and second subscriptions** (E3-10, 22) breach the "I bought a thing once" mental model and generate the angriest rows captured.

## Q9 — What makes people actually pay rather than use free information

1. **Being expected, not information:** "the only method I've ever found that has consistently gotten me in the gym" (human-coached app, E3-29); runners pay because they "don't trust themselves" to follow a free plan (E3-27). **Accountability is the tipping point.**
2. **Adaptation is the second differentiator:** a coach/plan that reschedules around injury and life beats static free PDFs (E3-27, 33) — our adaptation engine sits exactly on this willingness-to-pay line.
3. **Structure with a date:** bounded blocks with an endpoint (a race plan; an 8-week challenge with scans, "absolutely worth it" at ~£450 — E3-33, 40) work as commitment devices. The purchase *is* the commitment.
4. **Curation as shortcut:** paying to skip "months of digging through blogs and YouTube" (E3-36); remorse arrives when the free substitute is discovered late (E3-35). Claims honesty about what's genuinely inside is churn prevention.
5. **The symmetrical failure:** a $510 programme with no check-ins failed *on accountability* — "a good program that keeps you accountable and motivated is worth it" (E3-28). People forgive price; they don't forgive absent attention.
6. **Free wins where it supplies structure + credibility** (NHS C25K, free 30-day yoga journeys — E3-38, 39): where a trusted free programme exists, willingness-to-pay collapses. *(Directly relevant to Q1: the fitness-beginner niche has a beloved free incumbent at the running end; guided strength does not.)*
7. **Price stacking is a veto:** users cancel liked products when the fee sits on top of an existing spend (E3-37).

## Q14 — Lengths and payment periods users understand naturally

1. **Users natively speak in programme blocks:** 30 days (accessible on-ramp), 8–9 weeks (a completable challenge/course), 12 weeks (the fitness-industry default — "long enough to adapt, short enough to stay motivated", trainer-side voice), 75–90 days (identity-level, known burnout risk) (E3-38…42). **The 12-week frame is natively legible** — though the user-side (vs trainer-side) preference evidence is thin (limitation logged).
2. **Programme length and billing period are different mental objects — mismatches cause the worst outrage:** a "28 Day Challenge" auto-renewing for two years; a 3-month selection billed as 12; a one-off book spawning a subscription (E3-25, 12, 22).
3. **Monthly is the default understood unit; annual is repeatedly not understood at purchase** ("I had no idea of it being an annual subscription"; "$12/month" charged as $144 — E3-43…45, 55). **Weekly billing is read as a scam signal** when discovered (E3-09, 47, 48).
4. **Quarterly/multi-month upfront bundles surprise people** (E3-03, 11): users don't narrate "quarterly" as a natural unit — they narrate "charged for months I didn't want". **This is the strongest public-evidence caution against the 12-week-term billing hypothesis** and compounds the adversarial finding R-20 (store billing periods are month-shaped). The term model survives only with extreme disclosure discipline — or shifts toward M1 one-off per programme, which maps cleanly onto finding Q14-1's "block" mental model.
5. **A pay-once escape hatch has real demand** (ownership preference; "no subscription" as a shopping filter; lifetime deals hunted — E3-19, 50…52) — but lifetime-buyer *regret* evidence was unretrievable (gap logged).
6. **Billing that outlives the goal is experienced as punishment for succeeding:** the canonical regulator case — users charged "even when the consumers met their goals or after they cancelled" (E3-53, verified fact) and "finish out the duration" complaints (E3-12). *(External confirmation for the week-13 honest-renewal design and F18's no-hostage rules.)*
